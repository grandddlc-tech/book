title: Station Seven
---
The last transmission from Station Seven arrived at 04:12 on a Tuesday, and for reasons nobody at the research institute could later explain, it was not flagged for review until Thursday. By then, the support vessel Halcyon was already on its way, and Dr Ingrid Vos, the institute's most senior marine biologist, had listened to the recording eleven times.

Station Seven was an underwater habitat moored at a depth of two hundred and forty metres in a Norwegian fjord, a cluster of pressurised modules where four scientists spent rotations of six weeks studying bioluminescent organisms that thrived in perpetual darkness. Communication was routine to the point of tedium: daily logs, equipment reports, the occasional complaint about the food. The final transmission was different. It consisted of ninety seconds of what sounded like breathing, slow and deliberate, followed by the voice of the station commander, Pieter Haas, saying in an oddly conversational tone: "We've counted again. There are five of us now. Please don't send anyone."

Ingrid had known Pieter for fifteen years. He was methodical to the point of pedantry, a man who had once filed a three-page report about a faulty coffee machine. He was not given to jokes, and he was certainly not given to cryptic messages.

The Halcyon reached the fjord at dusk. The water was black and glassy, the cliffs on either side so steep that the sky above was reduced to a narrow, bruised strip. Ingrid descended in the transfer capsule with two technicians, Arne and Sofie, watching the depth gauge tick upward while the external lights illuminated nothing but drifting particles, like snow falling in reverse.

The docking procedure was uneventful. The hatch opened onto the main module, which was lit, warm, and immaculately tidy. Coffee cups had been washed and stacked. The logbook lay open on the central table, the last entry written in Pieter's precise hand on the previous Monday: "Routine sampling completed. All well." There was no sign of a struggle, and there was no sign of anyone.

They searched every module. In the sleeping quarters, four bunks were neatly made. A fifth sleeping bag had been unrolled on the floor between them. It was not part of the station's inventory.

Sofie found the video archive. The station's internal cameras recorded continuously, and the footage from the final week told a story that grew stranger with each viewing. On the first day, the four scientists went about their work as usual. On the second, they paused occasionally, as if listening. On the third, they could be seen counting, pointing to one another in turn and mouthing numbers. By the fifth day, they had stopped working altogether. They sat around the central table in silence, and every few hours they counted again, and each time their faces betrayed a mounting, inarticulate dread.

What unsettled Ingrid most was that the camera showed only four people. Always four. Yet in the final recordings, they were behaving, unmistakably, as though there were a fifth person at the table: passing a cup to an empty chair, leaning aside to make room, falling silent as if interrupted.

The last piece of footage, timestamped 04:11 on Tuesday, showed Pieter standing alone in front of the airlock that led to the diving chamber. He turned towards the camera and appeared to listen to someone just out of frame. Then he nodded, almost apologetically, opened the inner door, and stepped inside. The other three followed him, one by one. The door closed. The diving chamber's sensors recorded that the outer hatch had opened, and then that it had sealed again.

Arne was the first to say they should leave. Nobody argued. They gathered the hard drives and the logbook and returned to the capsule with a haste none of them acknowledged aloud. As the hatch sealed and the winch began to haul them towards the surface, Ingrid felt the cramped space settle around her, the three of them knee to knee in the dim red light.

It was Sofie who began, quite unconsciously, to count. Ingrid saw her lips moving and recognised, with a cold contraction in her stomach, the same gesture she had watched on the screen. Sofie pointed at Arne, then at Ingrid, then at herself, and then her finger drifted, trembling, towards the empty corner of the capsule.

Her mouth formed the word "four."

Above them, very faintly, something that was not the winch began to breathe.
