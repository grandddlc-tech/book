title: Evolution's Repeated Inventions
---
If one could rewind the tape of life and play it again, would anything familiar emerge? The palaeontologist Stephen Jay Gould famously argued that it would not. Evolution, in his view, is so contingent, so dependent on chance events such as asteroid impacts and random mutations, that a replay would produce a world utterly unlike our own. Yet a substantial body of evidence suggests that Gould may have overstated the case. Across the history of life, unrelated organisms have repeatedly arrived at strikingly similar solutions to the same problems, a phenomenon biologists call convergent evolution.

The classic illustration is the eye. Vertebrates such as humans and cephalopods such as octopuses both possess camera-type eyes, complete with a lens that focuses light onto a light-sensitive retina. Their last common ancestor, which lived more than five hundred million years ago, almost certainly had nothing more sophisticated than a patch of light-sensitive cells. The two designs therefore evolved independently. Tellingly, they differ in one revealing detail: in vertebrate eyes, the nerve fibres run across the front of the retina and must exit through a gap, creating a blind spot, whereas the octopus eye is wired more logically, with nerves behind the photoreceptors. Convergence produces similarity in function, not identity in construction.

Flight offers another example. Powered flight has evolved at least four times: in insects, in the extinct pterosaurs, in birds and in bats. Each lineage built its wings from different raw materials. Insect wings are outgrowths of the exoskeleton; pterosaurs stretched a membrane from an enormously elongated fourth finger; birds use feathers attached to fused finger bones; bats spread skin between several elongated fingers. Physics imposes the problem, aerodynamic lift, but evolutionary history determines the tools available to solve it.

Convergence extends far beyond anatomy. Echolocation, the ability to navigate by emitting sounds and interpreting the echoes, evolved separately in bats and toothed whales. Remarkably, genetic studies have found that some of the same genes, including one involved in the sensitivity of hair cells in the inner ear, underwent parallel changes in both groups. This finding suggests that convergence can operate not only at the level of visible traits but also at the level of molecules.

Perhaps the most whimsical example is what biologists have jokingly termed "carcinisation": the repeated evolution of crab-like body forms among crustaceans. True crabs, hermit crabs and king crabs are not closely related, yet several lineages have independently acquired the same compact, flattened shape with a tail tucked beneath the body. Researchers speculate that this arrangement offers advantages in protection and mobility, though the precise reasons remain debated.

Plants provide equally compelling cases. The succulent, spiny form we associate with cacti in the Americas evolved separately in certain euphorbias of Africa, to the point that the two are frequently confused in garden centres. Both are responses to the same environmental pressure: the need to store water and deter thirsty herbivores in arid landscapes.

Why does convergence occur so often? The principal explanation is that the number of viable solutions to any given problem is limited. The laws of physics, the properties of light and water, and the constraints of biochemistry narrow the range of designs that actually work. When similar pressures act on different lineages for long enough, natural selection tends to guide them towards the same peaks in the landscape of possibilities. The biologist Simon Conway Morris has argued, in direct opposition to Gould, that convergence makes certain outcomes, possibly including intelligence, close to inevitable.

This debate is not merely academic. If convergence is pervasive, it lends some predictability to evolution, with implications for fields as diverse as medicine and astrobiology. Bacteria under antibiotic attack, for instance, often evolve resistance through the same mutations, allowing scientists to anticipate threats. And if life exists on other planets, convergence suggests it might display familiar features, such as eyes, limbs or wings, even if built from entirely different chemistry.

Gould's insight that history matters remains valid; chance undeniably shapes the details of life. But the recurring patterns of convergence suggest that evolution is not a lottery with infinite outcomes. Instead, it resembles an inventor repeatedly rediscovering the same good ideas, constrained by the stubborn realities of the physical world.
