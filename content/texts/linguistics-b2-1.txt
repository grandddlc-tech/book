title: How Babies Crack the Code
---
Learning a new language as an adult is hard work. We study grammar tables, memorise vocabulary lists, and still struggle to understand fast conversations. Yet every healthy baby in the world learns at least one language without any lessons at all. By the age of five, most children can understand and produce complex sentences, and they do it without ever being taught a single grammar rule. How do they manage it?

The process begins earlier than most people think. Research shows that babies start learning about language before they are even born. In the last months of pregnancy, the unborn baby can hear sounds from outside the womb, especially the mother's voice. Studies have found that newborn babies prefer the sound of their mother's voice to other voices, and they prefer the language their mother spoke during pregnancy to an unfamiliar language. Some research even suggests that the melody of newborns' cries is influenced by the language they heard in the womb.

In their first months, babies are what some researchers call "citizens of the world". They can distinguish between almost all the speech sounds used in the world's languages, including sounds that their parents cannot hear as different. For example, Japanese adults often find it difficult to distinguish between the English sounds "r" and "l", but Japanese babies of six months can easily tell them apart. By the end of their first year, however, this ability begins to narrow. Babies become specialists in the sounds of the language around them and gradually lose sensitivity to sounds that are not important in that language.

At around six months, babies begin to babble, producing repeated syllables such as "ba-ba-ba" or "da-da-da". Babbling may sound meaningless, but it is an important form of practice. Deaf babies who are exposed to sign language even "babble" with their hands, producing repetitive movements that resemble signs. This suggests that babbling is not simply about making noise but about learning the building blocks of language, whatever form it takes.

One of the most difficult challenges for a baby is to find where words begin and end. In normal speech, we do not pause between words; they flow together in a continuous stream. Researchers have discovered that babies solve this problem by paying attention to statistics. In a famous experiment in 1996, psychologist Jenny Saffran played eight-month-old babies a stream of invented syllables with no pauses. Certain syllables always appeared together, while others combined only occasionally. After just two minutes, the babies could tell the difference between the "words" that always appeared together and random combinations. Without realising it, they were calculating probabilities.

Most children say their first word around their first birthday. For several months, vocabulary grows slowly, and then, at around eighteen months, many children experience a "vocabulary explosion", sometimes learning several new words a day. Soon they begin combining words into short phrases like "more milk" or "daddy gone".

Children's mistakes reveal a great deal about how they learn. A child who says "I goed to the park" or "two mouses" has never heard an adult say these things. Instead, the child has worked out a general rule, such as adding "-ed" for the past tense, and is applying it to every verb, including irregular ones. These errors show that children are not simply copying what they hear; they are actively discovering the patterns of their language.

Scientists still debate how much of this ability is innate. The linguist Noam Chomsky argued that humans are born with a "universal grammar", a built-in knowledge of the basic structure that all languages share. Other researchers believe that children learn language mainly through powerful general learning abilities and rich social interaction. Most experts today agree that both biology and environment play important roles.

What is clear is that interaction matters. Studies have shown that babies learn sounds from a live person far better than from a video or audio recording of the same person. Talking with babies, responding to their sounds, reading together, and singing all support language development. The quantity and quality of conversation children experience in their early years is linked to their later vocabulary and reading skills.

So the next time you struggle with a new language, remember that you were once an extraordinary language learner. As a baby, you cracked one of the most complex codes in the world, and you did it while learning to walk.
