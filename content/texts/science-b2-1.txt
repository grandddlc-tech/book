title: Inside a Lightning Bolt
---
Every second, somewhere on Earth, lightning strikes around forty to fifty times. That adds up to well over a billion flashes a year. For most of human history, people explained lightning as the anger of the gods. Today, scientists understand much of what happens inside a thunderstorm, although some parts of the process are still surprisingly mysterious.

Lightning begins with ice. Inside a tall storm cloud, called a cumulonimbus, strong currents of air carry water droplets upward, where the temperature is far below freezing. The droplets turn into small ice crystals and softer pellets of ice known as graupel. As these particles are thrown around by the wind, they crash into each other millions of times. Each collision transfers a tiny electric charge. In general, the lighter ice crystals become positively charged and rise to the top of the cloud, while the heavier graupel becomes negatively charged and stays lower down.

The result is a cloud that acts like a giant battery, with a positive top and a negative bottom. The ground below, meanwhile, becomes positively charged in response. Air is normally a good insulator, which means it does not allow electricity to flow easily. But when the difference in charge becomes large enough, the air can no longer hold it back.

What happens next takes only a fraction of a second. First, an invisible channel of charged air, called a stepped leader, moves down from the cloud in a series of short jumps, each about fifty metres long. At the same time, objects on the ground, such as trees, towers, and even people, may send up their own weak channels, called streamers. When a leader and a streamer meet, a complete path is formed, and a powerful current rushes through it. This is the bright flash we see, known as the return stroke. Interestingly, it actually travels upward, from the ground to the cloud, although it happens far too quickly for our eyes to notice.

The return stroke is extremely hot. For a brief moment, the air inside the channel reaches about 30,000 degrees Celsius, roughly five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. This sudden heat makes the air expand explosively, creating a shock wave that we hear as thunder. Because light travels much faster than sound, we always see the flash before we hear the thunder. You can estimate how far away a storm is by counting the seconds between the two: every three seconds equals approximately one kilometre.

Despite its power, lightning does not always hit the ground. In fact, most lightning stays inside the cloud or jumps between clouds. Only about a quarter of all flashes reach the Earth's surface. When they do, however, the effects can be serious. Lightning causes forest fires, damages buildings, and kills a significant number of people each year, mostly those who are outdoors during storms.

Fortunately, simple precautions greatly reduce the risk. The safest place during a thunderstorm is inside a solid building or a car with a metal roof, which carries the current around the people inside. Standing under a tall tree is dangerous, since lightning often strikes the highest object in an area. Experts also warn that lightning can strike up to fifteen kilometres away from the centre of a storm, so if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be hit.

Scientists continue to study lightning with high-speed cameras, satellites, and special radio sensors. One open question is how lightning actually starts. The electric fields measured inside clouds seem too weak to break down the air on their own. Some researchers believe that cosmic rays, high-energy particles from space, may provide the initial spark. Others are investigating strange flashes above thunderstorms, with names like red sprites and blue jets, which reach high into the atmosphere.

Lightning may be one of the most familiar natural events, yet it continues to surprise us. Each flash is a reminder that even the most common things in nature can hide complex and fascinating science.
