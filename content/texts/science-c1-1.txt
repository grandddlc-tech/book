title: The Tyranny of the Second
---
Few units of measurement are as taken for granted as the second. We glance at our phones, catch trains, and schedule meetings with barely a thought for what a second actually is. Yet the question of how to define it precisely has occupied scientists for centuries, and the answer has shifted from the heavens to the interior of the atom, with consequences that reach far beyond the laboratory.

For most of history, time was anchored to the motion of celestial bodies. A day was one rotation of the Earth, and the second was simply a convenient subdivision: one eighty-six-thousand-four-hundredth of a mean solar day. This definition served admirably for navigation and astronomy until the early twentieth century, when increasingly accurate mechanical and quartz clocks revealed an awkward truth. The Earth is an unreliable timekeeper. Its rotation is gradually slowing, largely because of tidal friction with the Moon, and it also fluctuates unpredictably owing to earthquakes, ocean currents, and shifts in the planet's molten core.

The solution emerged from quantum physics. Atoms of a given element absorb and emit electromagnetic radiation at extraordinarily specific frequencies, determined by the energy differences between their electron states. Crucially, these frequencies are identical for every atom of that element, everywhere in the universe. An atom, in other words, is a natural pendulum that never wears out and never varies. In 1955, physicists at the National Physical Laboratory in Britain built the first practical caesium atomic clock, and in 1967 the international scientific community formally redefined the second as the duration of 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the radiation associated with a particular transition in the caesium-133 atom.

The precision of atomic clocks has since improved at a staggering rate. The best caesium fountain clocks would neither gain nor lose a second in roughly three hundred million years. Newer optical clocks, which use atoms such as strontium or ytterbium that oscillate at far higher frequencies, are more accurate still: some would drift by less than a second over the entire age of the universe. Such clocks are so sensitive that they can detect the difference in the flow of time caused by raising them a few centimetres, a direct demonstration of Einstein's prediction that time passes more slowly in stronger gravitational fields.

This extraordinary accuracy is not merely an academic curiosity. Satellite navigation systems such as GPS depend entirely on it. Each satellite carries atomic clocks and continuously broadcasts the time; a receiver calculates its position by comparing signals from several satellites. Since radio signals travel at the speed of light, an error of a single microsecond would translate into a positional error of around three hundred metres. Engineers must even correct for relativity, because clocks on orbiting satellites tick at a slightly different rate from those on the ground. Financial markets, electricity grids, and telecommunications networks likewise rely on synchronised atomic time to coordinate transactions and transmissions measured in millionths of a second.

The divorce between atomic time and astronomical time has, however, created an unexpected administrative headache. Because the Earth's rotation does not keep pace with atomic clocks, the two gradually drift apart. To reconcile them, timekeepers introduced the "leap second" in 1972, an extra second occasionally inserted into the world's official time scale. Twenty-seven have been added so far. Leap seconds are notoriously disruptive for computer systems, which often assume that every minute contains exactly sixty seconds; several have triggered outages at major websites and airlines. In 2022, after years of debate, the international body responsible for weights and measures voted to abolish the leap second by 2035, effectively accepting that civil time will slowly diverge from the rhythm of the sun.

Meanwhile, metrologists are preparing for yet another redefinition. Optical clocks have now surpassed caesium so decisively that the current standard has become a bottleneck, and a new definition based on optical transitions is expected within the next decade. Choosing which atom to enshrine is itself a delicate scientific and diplomatic negotiation.

The history of the second, then, is a history of humanity's growing ambition to measure the world with ever greater fidelity. It is also a reminder that even our most fundamental units are human conventions, refined, contested, and occasionally abandoned as our understanding of nature deepens.
