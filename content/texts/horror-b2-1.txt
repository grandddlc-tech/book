title: The Night Guard
---
When Daniel accepted the job as night guard at the Holloway Museum of Natural History, his friends thought it was the most boring job in the world. Twelve hours alone in a building full of stuffed animals and old bones. But Daniel needed the money, and he liked the quiet. He brought books, a flask of coffee, and headphones, and for the first month nothing happened at all.

The museum had three floors. The ground floor held the dinosaur skeletons, including a huge Tyrannosaurus whose head nearly touched the glass ceiling. The first floor was full of birds and mammals behind glass, frozen in the middle of running, hunting, or sleeping. The top floor, which was closed to the public, stored thousands of objects in wooden drawers: insects, fossils, and animal skulls collected over two hundred years.

Daniel's routine was simple. Every two hours he walked through each floor with his torch, checked the doors, and wrote the time in a notebook. The previous guard, an old man named Mr Pike, had left a note taped to the front of the notebook. It said: "Always finish your round on the top floor before 3 a.m. Never be up there at 3." Daniel assumed it was a joke for new guards. He laughed and forgot about it.

One night in November, a storm knocked out the electricity for a few minutes. When the emergency lights came on, Daniel noticed that the clock on the wall had stopped, and he had lost track of time. He grabbed his torch and began his round. The dinosaurs looked strange in the red emergency light, their shadows stretching across the floor. On the first floor, he had the uncomfortable feeling that the glass eyes of the animals were following him, but he told himself it was only the light.

He reached the top floor and began walking between the tall wooden cabinets. The air was colder up there, and it smelled of dust and old paper. Somewhere in the dark, he heard a soft sound, like a drawer sliding open. He stopped. The sound came again, closer this time.

"Hello?" he called. His voice sounded very small.

He moved his torch slowly along the rows. At the end of the corridor, one drawer was open. Inside, on a bed of yellow cotton, lay a collection of bird skulls, each one labelled in neat, old-fashioned handwriting. As he watched, one of the labels moved slightly, as if someone had breathed on it.

Then Daniel noticed his watch. It said 2:59.

He turned to leave, walking fast, then faster. Behind him, he heard another drawer open, and another, and another, until the whole floor seemed to be filled with the sound of wood sliding against wood. He did not look back. He ran down the stairs, past the staring animals, past the great skeleton, and did not stop until he reached the security office and locked the door.

At exactly 3:00, the noise stopped.

Daniel sat in the office until the sun came up, his hands shaking. When the morning staff arrived, he went upstairs with the head of the museum, a calm woman called Dr Shaw. Every drawer on the top floor was closed. Nothing was missing. Nothing had moved.

"You probably fell asleep," she said kindly. "Night shifts do strange things to the mind."

But as they walked back towards the stairs, Daniel noticed something on the floor near the cabinet where the first drawer had opened. It was a small paper label, the kind used for the bird skulls. He picked it up. In the same neat, old-fashioned handwriting, it said: "D. Morgan. Collected November 14th."

Daniel's surname was Morgan. And it was the fourteenth of November.

He handed in his notice that afternoon. A week later, curiosity got the better of him, and he called Mr Pike to ask about the note. The old man was silent for a long time. Then he said, "I worked there for thirty years. I always finished my round by three. The guard before me didn't." He paused. "They say he was a great collector."

Daniel never found out what happened to the label. He was sure he had put it in his coat pocket. But when he looked for it later, the pocket was empty, except for a tiny, white feather.
